## Origins

The Indus Valley Civilization emerged in the northern Indian subcontinent, centered along the Indus River basin. Its earliest traces date to around 7000 BCE, with the Pre‑Harappan phase lasting until about 5500 BCE, followed by Early Harappan (5500–2800 BCE), Mature Harappan (2800–1900 BCE), Late Harappan (1900–1500 BCE), and Post‑Harappan (1500–600 BCE) [1]. The civilization spread over roughly 1,500 km of riverbanks and extended into present‑day Pakistan, India, Afghanistan, and Nepal, covering a territory of about 900 miles [1]. Major urban centers such as Harappa and Mohenjo‑daro rose during the Mature phase, each housing 40,000–50,000 inhabitants, a remarkable size for the era [1]. The decline began around 1900 BCE, likely due to climatic shifts, river course changes, and economic factors, though the exact causes remain uncertain [1].


## Urban Planning

The Indus Valley cities were laid out on a grid, with streets intersecting at right angles and wide avenues that facilitated movement and trade. Each block contained standardized rectangular houses built from fired bricks, often with flat roofs and a central courtyard. The streets were paved with compacted earth or baked bricks, and drainage channels ran parallel to the main roads, channeling rainwater into a system of covered drains that led to the river or a central reservoir. The houses featured a private kitchen and a public bathing area, indicating a blend of domestic and communal life. Public buildings, such as the Great Bath, were centrally located and constructed from finely cut stone, suggesting civic planning and a focus on hygiene and ritual. The uniformity of the layout and the sophisticated drainage system reflect a highly organized urban administration that coordinated construction, sanitation, and water management across the city. ## Socio‑Economic Structure

The Indus Valley civilization organized its economy around extensive trade networks that linked its cities to Mesopotamia, the Arabian Peninsula, and the Indus Delta, facilitating the exchange of lapis lazuli, carnelian, and textiles [1]. Craft specialization was evident in workshops producing bronze tools, pottery, and beadwork, with evidence of guild‑like production units that maintained quality standards and standardized weights [1]. Social organization appears to have been stratified yet relatively egalitarian; the uniformity of housing and the absence of monumental palaces suggest a communal governance structure, while elite burials and specialized craft areas indicate a class of skilled artisans and traders [1]. These elements together reveal a complex society that balanced collective planning with specialized production and long‑distance commerce.
